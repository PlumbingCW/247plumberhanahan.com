Sewer Camera Inspection in Hanahan, SC

A sewer camera inspection in Hanahan, SC shows the inside of the line, which no walkthrough can. Hanahan 24/7 Plumbing offers it with licensed plumbers and same-day appointments when available.

A sewer camera inspection sends a small waterproof camera on a flexible rod through the drain line to record its condition from the house to the street connection. The camera shows what the pipe is made of, whether it is cracked or sagging, and whether roots or debris are inside.

Most people book it for a reason: buying or selling a home, a repeat backup, or planned digging. Each reason asks a slightly different question of the footage.

Buying or selling a home: what a camera shows that a walkthrough cannot

A house can look perfect and have a sewer line that is cracked, sagging or full of roots. None of that shows up at a showing or in a standard home inspection of the visible plumbing. The camera shows it in the pipe itself.

For a buyer, the footage is a cost question: does the line need work soon, and who should pay? For a seller, a clean run can answer a buyer's questions before they arise. If the camera finds a defect, ask your agent or attorney how it affects the contract, and ask us what the repair involves.

Book the camera early. Findings, repair quotes and any talk with the other side take time, and a result that arrives the day before closing leaves no room to use it.

What the footage can show

These are the findings a plumber looks for.

Cracks and breaks

A line or crack along the pipe wall, or a joint that has pulled apart.

Roots

Fine hairs at a joint or a thick mat filling the pipe.

Bellies and sags

A low section that holds water and collects solids.

Offsets

Two pipe ends that no longer line up.

Corrosion and scale

Rough, flaking interior, typical of cast iron with age.

Material and connections

Whether the pipe is cast iron, PVC or ABS, and where branches join.

The equipment: camera head, locator and recorded footage

Callers sometimes ask whether a plumber really has the equipment. A proper inspection uses a camera head on a flexible push rod, often with a self-leveling feature so the picture stays upright as the head turns, plus lights to see the pipe wall.

A locator pairs with a small transmitter in or behind the camera head, so the plumber can find the head from above ground and read its depth. We record the run, so the footage can be reviewed with the plumber, a buyer, or a repair contractor. Ask for the recording when you book.

How an inspection goes

This is the usual order of a visit.

  1. 1

    Find the access point

    A cleanout is best. Without one, we may enter through a pulled toilet or a roof vent.

  2. 2

    Check the line is clear enough

    A heavily blocked line may need cleaning first, and we will tell you before we begin.

  3. 3

    Run the camera

    The head moves through the line while we watch a monitor and note footage markers at each finding.

  4. 4

    Locate defects

    The locator marks the surface above anything we find, with its depth.

  5. 5

    Review the results

    We describe what we saw and point out the findings that matter.

Reading what the camera finds

A finding does not always mean a repair.

FindingWhat it usually meansTypical next step
Fine roots at one jointEarly intrusion through a small gapCleaning now, monitoring or a spot repair
Heavy scale on cast ironAge-related corrosionCleaning, then watching, or replacement if walls are thin
Standing water in a low sectionA belly that collects solidsRepair of that section
Cracked or offset jointA leak point and root entry pathSleeve, section replacement or lining
Clean, round pipeNo visible defect on that runNone, keep the footage

The camera shows condition on the day. It does not predict when a pipe will fail.

Before the inspection: access and questions

Preparation makes the visit shorter and the answers clearer.

  • Find the cleanout and clear plants or boxes from around it.
  • Tell us about past backups, slow drains and any work done on the line.
  • Note the age of the house and what you know about the pipe material.
  • Ask the seller or listing agent for access and permission if you are buying.
  • Decide who will receive the footage and report.
  • Write down your questions: depth, material, and anything a buyer would ask.

What a camera cannot tell you

A camera cannot see through a full blockage, so a plugged line must be cleared first. It does not show the public sewer beyond your connection if the cleanout is near the house. It also cannot test water pressure or measure how a pipe behaves under heavy flow.

Footage is a snapshot. A line that looks fine today may be clogged in a year if roots are active. For a full picture of a problem, we pair the camera with cleaning, and our hydro jetting page explains when scouring helps. If the footage shows damage, sewer line repair covers the options.

Finding the line before you dig or build

Planning a fence, a patio, a pool or a new tree? A locator can trace the sewer line from above ground and mark its path and depth. That helps you avoid hitting it and avoid planting roots over it.

Call 811 before any dig, because the one-call center handles locate requests for buried utilities. A plumber's locator can trace your own sewer line from above ground. If you are in Goose Creek or nearby, we can do the same, and the sewer provider can tell you where the public connection is.

Scheduling

An inspection is planned work, and we offer same-day appointments when available. If sewage is backing up now, call as an emergency and see our sewer line cleaning page instead.

What affects the price of a sewer camera inspection

Inspection cost depends on the line, not just the camera.

Line length
A short run to the street costs less than a long one.
Access
A cleanout makes the visit simple. Without one, the plumber may need to pull a toilet.
Cleaning first
A line packed with grease or roots has to be cleared before the camera can pass.
Locating and marking
Tracing depth and position adds time but pays off before any digging.
Recorded report
Providing the footage and a written summary takes extra time.

Home insurance usually does not pay for an inspection that is done for a purchase or for routine checks. Some policies help with a repair after a sudden event, so ask your carrier.

Frequently asked questions

Does a sewer camera inspection work for a home I am buying?

Yes. It is a common request at that stage. Get the seller's permission for access, ask for the recording, and bring the findings to your agent or attorney.

How do I know the plumber has the right equipment?

Ask what they use. A proper setup is a camera head on a push rod, lights, a monitor, a locator and a way to record. Ask whether you will receive the footage.

Can you tell me how deep my sewer line is?

Yes, a locator reads depth from the surface. We also mark the path for digging, and you should still call 811 before any excavation so buried utilities are located.

What if the camera cannot get through?

A blockage or a collapsed section can stop it. We tell you where it stopped and what likely caused it, then suggest cleaning or repair.

Is an inspection the same as a leak detection visit?

No. The camera looks inside the drain line. Leak detection finds leaks in pressurized water lines, and our leak detection page explains that service.

How long is the footage useful?

It shows the pipe on the day of the run. If roots are active or the line has been repaired since, the picture changes, so treat old footage as history, not a current report.

Can you locate my sewer cleanout or line?

Yes, with a locator. If there is no cleanout, we can tell you where one should go.
